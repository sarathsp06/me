We're taking our family — three adults, a five-year-old, and a one-year-old — on a road trip from Rotterdam to Switzerland in May 2026. Seven days, four stops, one loop through the Black Forest, Rhine Falls, and the Bernese Oberland. Waterfalls first, mountains second — that's the priority. I've spent weeks planning this route to keep every driving leg under five hours, avoid steep hikes, and build in enough flexibility that nobody melts down (adults included). This post is the full plan — day-by-day itinerary, costs, packing lists, and everything I wish someone had written down before I started researching.

**Priority hierarchy:** Waterfalls > Mountains (cable car/cog railway) > Caves > Lakes (boats, promenades) > Cities

<div class="prose-content">

Our route heads south from Rotterdam through Germany — straight to Triberg for Germany's highest waterfall, then Rhine Falls (Europe's largest by volume) on the way into Switzerland. Four nights in Diemtigen — a quiet alpine valley in the Bernese Oberland — with day trips to Lauterbrunnen Valley and Niesen or Niederhorn. Then back north via Königswinter on the Rhine. The first leg is the longest at five hours; after that, every drive stays under four.

<Callout type="insight" title="Weather-based day swapping" icon="fa-cloud-sun">
  <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Days 3 and 5 (mountain day and relax day) can be swapped to chase the best weather. Clear days go to Niesen or Niederhorn (need views). Grey or rainy days go to Lake Thun or Lauterbrunnen — Trümmelbach Falls is underground and Staubbach is dramatic in rain. Triberg Waterfalls work in any weather — more spectacular in rain.</p>
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Supply strategy" icon="fa-shopping-cart">
  <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Day 1 (Saturday) is a long drive to Triberg — <strong>stock up at a motorway DM/Rossmann en route or in Triberg Saturday evening before shops close (~8 PM).</strong> Day 2 (Sunday) has shops closed. Critical: buy nappies, baby food, and medicine at DM/Rossmann in Germany — Swiss prices are 40–60% higher. Diemtigen has minimal shops — do a Coop/Migros run in Spiez or Thun on Day 3. Re-stock in Germany on Day 6 (Königswinter/Bonn area).</p>
</Callout>

    <p class="text-sm text-gray-600 leading-relaxed"><strong>~45min drive each way</strong> via Spiez and Interlaken. The Lauterbrunnen Valley is one of the deepest glacial valleys in the Alps — 72 waterfalls pour off vertical cliffs. Two headline stops:</p>

    <p class="text-sm text-gray-600 leading-relaxed mt-2"><strong>Staubbach Falls</strong> — 297m free-falling waterfall visible from the village. Completely free. Flat walk from parking to the viewpoint. One of the most photographed waterfalls in Switzerland.</p>

<SectionHeading icon="fa-mountain" color="emerald">Diemtigen: The Main Base</SectionHeading>

<div class="prose-content">

Four nights in Diemtigen is the heart of the trip. A quiet alpine valley in the Bernese Oberland — farmland, forests, and streams. No tourist crowds. Significantly cheaper than Lake Lucerne villages, and closer to Lauterbrunnen. The Diemtigtal is a designated Swiss landscape park with flat valley-floor walks perfect for strollers and seniors.

</div>

<SectionHeading icon="fa-circle-question" color="purple">FAQ</SectionHeading>

<div class="space-y-4 my-6">
  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Do I need a Swiss motorway vignette?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Yes. Any car using Swiss motorways needs a vignette (CHF 40, valid for the calendar year). You can buy it online at <a href="https://www.e-vignette.ch/en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-blue-600 underline">e-vignette.ch</a>, at border petrol stations, or at Swiss post offices. Fines for not having one are steep (CHF 200+).</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Why Niesen or Niederhorn instead of Pilatus or Rigi?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600"><strong>Cost and convenience.</strong> Pilatus golden round trip costs CHF 111/adult and requires being on Lake Lucerne. Rigi is CHF 72/adult. Niesen (CHF 56) and Niederhorn (CHF 44) give similar panoramic summit experiences at a fraction of the cost, and they're 15-35min from Diemtigen. The views are different — Niesen gives you the full Jungfrau massif from the south, which is arguably more dramatic than the Lake Lucerne panorama. Niederhorn has marmots.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Why Diemtigen instead of Lake Lucerne?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600"><strong>Three reasons: cost, proximity, and calm.</strong> Diemtigen accommodation is €70-120/night vs €150-250 at Lake Lucerne. It's 45min to Lauterbrunnen (vs 1h15 from Lake Lucerne). And it's a quiet alpine valley — better for family rest days, naps, and a slower pace. The tradeoff is you lose the Lake Lucerne steamer experience — but you gain Lake Thun (equally scenic, less crowded) nearby.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Is Trümmelbach Falls senior-friendly?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Mostly yes. A tunnel lift takes you up inside the mountain — no climbing required. The interior walkways are uneven and wet, so wear grippy shoes and go slowly. The spray is heavy in places. It's not strenuous, but it's not a flat promenade either. Seniors with reasonable mobility will manage fine. If in doubt, the lower falls (accessible from the lift) are the most impressive — you can skip the upper levels.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Can I take a stroller on the Niesen funicular?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Yes. The Niesen funicular accommodates strollers (unfolded). The summit terrace is flat and stroller-friendly. Same for the Niederhorn cable car. <strong>Exception:</strong> Triberg Waterfalls and Trümmelbach Falls require a baby carrier — trails are natural/narrow/wet.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Why stock up in Germany before crossing into Switzerland?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Swiss prices for everyday items (nappies, baby food, medicine, snacks) are 40–60% higher than Germany. DM and Rossmann are the best drugstores — stock up at a motorway service station on Day 1 or in Triberg Saturday evening. You'll re-enter Germany on Day 6, so you can top up again then.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Are there shops in Diemtigen?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Minimal — Diemtigen is a small village. For proper grocery shopping (Coop, Migros), drive to <strong>Spiez</strong> (~20min) or <strong>Thun</strong> (~25min). Plan a supply run on Day 3 (mountain day afternoon) or Day 5 (relax day). Self-catering in the apartment saves a lot on food costs.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">What currency does Switzerland use?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Swiss Francs (CHF). Cards are widely accepted in towns, restaurants, and transport. Smaller villages and parking meters sometimes need cash. ATMs are available at train stations and in larger towns. Some places near the border accept EUR but give change in CHF at a poor rate — better to use CHF.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="bg-white border border-gray-200 rounded-lg">
    <summary class="cursor-pointer px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-gray-800 hover:bg-gray-50">Are shops open on Saturday evening and Sunday in Germany?</summary>
    <div class="px-4 pb-3">
      <p class="text-sm text-gray-600">Mostly no. German shops close early on Saturday afternoon and are closed on Sunday. Your return passes through Königswinter on Thursday (Day 6) — shops will be open, so stock up then. Petrol station shops are the exception — they're open on weekends but pricier.</p>
    </div>
  </details>
